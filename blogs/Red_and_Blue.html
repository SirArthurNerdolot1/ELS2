Red and Blue

November 27, 2020

I remember when life was more complicated. When people were allowed to be nuanced, and could have multiple, overlapping identities. When people could depend upon their family for unconditional love, whether they were Red or Blue. Back when you could leave your house, and wander aimlessly without half the people you met eyeing you with suspicion. Then the Reds won the elections, and changed the laws to keep themselves perpetually in power. Or did they just use the same Emergency laws that their predecessors, the Blues, had had used to quash dissidents? Ever since then power has changed hands like clockwork, between the two in a series of revolutions and counter revolutions. No one even remembers anymore. It doesn’t even matter anymore. I’ve heard from old-timers about how in their day, people would discriminate on the basis of stupid things like religion. Nothing like that exists anymore. One’s affiliation, Red or Blue, is marked in childhood, and then nothing else matters. Familial bonds are washed away as the concerned Party takes charge of the child’s “education”. Children go to either Red schools or Blue schools. But their days are spent in the same way, reciting the names and birthdates of prominent leaders of their respective factions. They attend weekly self-criticism sessions, where they repent for their actions over the past week which may have been even slightly sympathetic to the other side. All rational thought has crumbled under the duress of unrelenting dogma. I come from a complicated place. It is a Red-majority area in a time when the Blues are in government. Talk of liberation and justice is common, even though these words are nothing but hollow shells now. Red leaders are frequently rounded up and promptly shipped off to prisons on the most frivolous of charges. Even the Blues live in fear of getting their throats slit by their milkmen or maids. If a Red on the street saw a Blue get injured in some horrific accident, they wouldn’t dare help him. Not when they could be seen by their fellow Reds. All of this vitriol was a result of the distrust of their own, as much their terror of the unknown. I used to work for a newspaper, you know. I was a Sub-Editor in the Objectivity Department. My job was to make sure that all the reports coming in were as close as possible to the truth. “Man, 35, Murdered by Neighbour” would be changed to “Red Brutally Murders Innocent Blue”. I think you can figure out for yourself which side my paper leaned toward. Often, it would extend to changing the details of a certain event. Although it wasn’t as if we had much of a choice. Reporters and editors alike who seemed to sympathise with the Reds had a mysterious habit of disappearing mysteriously. It could be something as minor as printing about delays in construction of a Blue project. So we did what was needed, and slowly what we wrote became the truth. This is the story of how I got swept in the tide of hate, yet managed to find my peace. The day it all started was an ordinary one, or as ordinary as it could be in that atmosphere of noxious hatred. I was at the Office, just after lunch break, during which I had eaten biryani from a nearby shop. Now, let me tell you about that particular shop. The owner was an old man, one who had seen governments and parties come and go; and all he cared about was keeping his business open and his family fed. When the centre was Blue, he would hang banners on his storefront which declared his loyalties. And when the tide turned, he knew how to adapt. He had two of every license and identification required to run a business. It was like that for a lot of people. After all, it didn’t really matter who you supported, as long as you did it loudly. Anyway, on that particular day, I sat in the Office, awash with soft amber 4 o’ clock sunlight. It was almost time for our daily pledge of allegiance to the Blues. That was when, all of a sudden the shouting started. I ducked just in time as a brick flew in from the window right next to me. I looked out from the first floor window to see absolute pandemonium on the streets. A huge crowd advanced from one end of the road, smashing through everything it passed. Shops were afire, as big men with lathis roamed about smashing windows and beating people. And the looters! They were everywhere, scurrying about like rats, gathering everything that they could. The man on the adjacent desk, Singh, was shouting hysterically, “What is going on?! Why are they doing this?”. This was no time to lose composure. Everyone knew what this was about. Later that evening, Das, a rising young Red leader, was about to deliver a speech at the festival grounds. Before sunrise, reports had started coming in of a big raid by the authorities at Das’ residence. They had found a lot of incriminating evidence, and apprehended him for “treasonous activities”. What those pieces of evidence and activities were, no one seemed to care. I can only imagine the madness that must have been unleashed as the Red protests turned violent, and met with Blue counter-protesters. During the commotion inside the Office, I turned a furtive glance outwards and noticed a mob forming at the entrance to the building. Quickly, I ripped off my Blue armband, and threw down my citizen ID as I rushed towards the back exit. A young clerk, who I did not recognise, must have noticed what was going on and had the good sense to follow me outside. And he did so just in time, for as we hurriedly headed out into the street, we could hear the crashes and the shouts as the mob broke into the Office. I had only one thing on my mind: survival. Once outside, I saw the charred remains of a torched biryani shop. The next few hours were chaos. I couldn’t go back home, the Reds in the neighbourhood must have obliterated it by then. The only real choice I had was to somehow escape into the countryside. Once the Military Police took hold of the city, there was no telling whose side they would take. Of course, being loyal to the Blue government made it seem obvious, but there was no telling if that government would be able to survive this. Things like this happened often, and hold on power was elusive, and fleeting for both sides. I turned to Ali, the young clerk who had been my companion upto now. Suddenly we were engulfed in a crowd. There were men with crowbars, hockey sticks, and basically anything that could be used to inflict injury. They were shouting things that made no sense, as such zealots were wont to do. I could make out “Justice for Das!” and the like; these must have been Reds. I noticed Ali still had his blue armband on. This was going to be ugly. I did what I had to. It was terrible. They were all around us, and there was a moment of expectant calm. It was not as if they were hesitating to kill us; it felt like a call to action for me. A quick punch to the face, and Ali was down on the ground. “You filthy Blue bastard!”. The words were coming out from my mouth, but I didn’t feel like I was the one doing this to Ali. In the next few moments, the mob descended on him as I tried to get lost in the crowd. I was nearing the city gates now, just a short distance from safety. That was when I saw it. I don’t even know who they were, Red or Blue, but they were trying to kill a child. A child. I remembered the torched shop. What they had done to Ali. What I had done to Ali. And I realised that the only way to make up for what I, and we as a society did upto now was to reverse this descent into madness. One act of fraternity at a time. No more boundaries, just love for the fellow man. Even though the city gates were in sight, there was no doubt in my mind. I was going to save that child’s life, even if it meant my death. I jumped into the throng.
